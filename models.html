The work

Models

Four classifiers plus a baseline, all fixed. Everyone compares the same models with the same settings: nothing is tuned. Logistic regression, random forest and gradient boosting are from the lecture; kNN is new. Your agent writes all five pipelines ? to this spec.

“Encode” is the supplied encode() step, which turns each text category into 0/1 columns, learned from the training rows only. “Impute” fills each missing value with the training median ?; “scale” puts every feature on the same scale ?. AGENT_BRIEF.md gives your agent the exact code-level spec.

The four classifiers plus a baseline

The yardstick

Prevalence baseline ?

Core idea: every new patient gets the same probability: the share of training patients who had the outcome.

  • training patient: had the outcome
  • training patient: didn't
  • new patient: prediction only

Limitation: it ranks no one above anyone else, so its AUROC is 0.5. It is the yardstick, not a model to use.

From the lecture

Logistic regression ?

Core idea: a weighted sum of the features plus an intercept gives each patient a score. An S-shaped curve (the sigmoid) maps that score to a probability between 0 and 1.

p = 11 + e−z where z = b0 + b1x1 + b2x2 + ⋯ + bkxk

The xs are a patient's features, the bs are the weights the model learns (b0 is the intercept), z is the score and p the predicted probability.

  • probability = 0.5: the threshold (left) and the decision boundary it makes (right)
  • had the outcome
  • didn't

Illustrative Made-up numbers. Move the sliders to tilt or slide the decision boundary and watch each patient's score move along the curve. The curve only converts scores into probabilities. Because the score is a weighted sum of the features, all the patients with probability 0.5 lie on a straight line (a flat surface, with more features): the activity's model has a straight decision boundary in its input feature space. Here you place the line by hand; the real model learns it from the training patients, choosing the weights that make its probabilities fit their outcomes best.

Limitation: it misses curved or combined effects unless you add them as features.

New

k-nearest neighbours (kNN) ?

Core idea: a new patient's probability is the share of positives among the k most similar training patients. In the activity, k is fixed at 25.

Why scaling matters
“Similar” means close in distance across all features. Without scaling, a feature measured in large units (length of stay in days, lab counts in the dozens) would swamp one measured in small units (a 0/1 flag). Scaling changes how distances are calculated so that no feature's units dominate. It does not make every feature equally informative: a feature that doesn't matter still adds noise to the distance.
The effect of k
A small k follows individual training patients closely, so its probabilities jump around. A large k averages more patients, so its probabilities are smoother but blur real differences. With k = 25, every probability is a multiple of 1/25 = 0.04.
Try it: drag a new patient and watch its neighbours

With scaling on, distances use scaled values, but the plot still shows each feature in its original units.

Limitation: it keeps the training patients rather than learning a formula, so it must find nearby training patients for each prediction, and it is easily misled by features that don't matter.

From the lecture

Random forest ?

Core idea: many decision trees; the probability is the average of the trees' probabilities. Each tree is grown on a resample of the training patients (drawn with replacement), and each split considers only a random subset of the candidate features, so the trees differ.

One new patient (made up): age 62, BMI 33, systolic blood pressure (BP) 128. The highlighted path is the route this patient takes through each tree.

Illustrative Forest probability = average of the trees = (0.20 + 0.40 + 0.30) ÷ 3 = 0.30. The activity's forest averages 300 trees.

Limitation: can overfit: compare its training and test AUROC in Section 3 ?. One prediction is hard to explain.

From the lecture

Gradient boosting ?

Core idea: trees added in sequence. Each new tree learns a correction intended to reduce the current model's loss (how wrong its predictions are on the training patients). The corrections add up on the score scale, and the final score becomes a probability.

  • starting and final score
  • tree raises the score
  • tree lowers the score

Illustrative One made-up patient. The start is the log-odds of the training prevalence, 0.20: ln(0.20 ÷ 0.80) = −1.39. Final score: −1.39 + 0.80 − 0.35 + 0.45 = −0.49, so the probability is 1 ÷ (1 + e0.49) = 0.38. Each correction is on the log-odds scale, not a probability, and a tree can lower a patient's prediction as well as raise it.

Limitation: with many trees it can fit noise in the training patients; it has several settings, all left at LightGBM's defaults here.

Common questions

Why can't my agent tune k?

Everyone compares the same fixed models, so differences come from the data and the comparison, not from tuning effort. Tuning is outside this activity.

check.py says a model doesn't match the spec.

Give your agent the failure line: it names the steps and settings that model must have. The fix belongs in analysis/models.py, as one pipeline per model; nothing may transform the data before training.
