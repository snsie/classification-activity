Glossary

Every term behind a ? button, in plain language and in the lecture's words.

Basics

Classification

Predicting a category (readmitted or not) rather than a number. Many classifiers first estimate a probability, then a threshold turns it into a class.

Two classes is binary classification, the focus of this activity.

Positive class

The outcome of interest: the event we want the model to find, such as “readmitted within 30 days”.

“Positive” does not mean good. Like a positive test result, it means the thing we were looking for was found. Name the positive class first: every metric except accuracy changes meaning if you flip it.

Prevalence

The share of patients in the positive class. For example, 9% of discharges were readmitted within 30 days.

Precision depends on prevalence: the same model gives fewer real flags out of every 100 when the outcome is rarer.

Estimated probability

The classifier's estimate of a patient's chance of the positive class, between 0 and 1.

It is an estimate. Whether a predicted 30% really means about 30 in 100 such patients needs its own check.

Threshold

The cut-off that turns a probability into a class: predict positive (“flag”) when the probability is at or above it.

0.50 is a choice people make, not something the model learns. Moving the threshold changes no probability and needs no retraining.

Accuracy

The fraction of predictions that were correct: (TP + TN) ÷ all patients.

Accuracy can be useful when the classes are roughly balanced and both kinds of mistake matter about equally. It cannot tell you which mistakes the model makes.

The accuracy trap

With imbalanced classes, a model that finds no one can still score high accuracy. If 91% were not readmitted, saying “no” to everyone is 91% accurate.

Always compare an accuracy with the “most common class for everyone” baseline.

“Most common class for everyone” baseline

The simplest rule there is: predict the most common class for every patient, such as “not readmitted”. Its accuracy is the share of that class.

Before trusting any accuracy number, compute what this rule scores.

Class imbalance

One class is much more common than the other, such as 9 readmitted for every 91 not.

The confusion matrix

Confusion matrix

A 2 × 2 table comparing what the model said with what happened. In this course, rows are the actual class and columns the predicted class.

Every patient lands in one of four groups: TP, FN, FP or TN. Correct results sit on the diagonal. scikit-learn's confusion_matrix(y, pred) uses another order ([[TN, FP], [FN, TP]]), so always read the labels before the numbers.

True positive (TP)

The model flagged the patient, and the patient had the outcome. A case caught.

False negative (FN)

The model did not flag the patient, but the patient had the outcome. A missed case.

False positive (FP)

The model flagged the patient, but the patient did not have the outcome. A false alarm.

True negative (TN)

The model did not flag the patient, and the patient did not have the outcome. Correctly left alone.

Sensitivity (recall, TPR)

Of the patients who actually had the outcome, the share the model flagged: TP ÷ (TP + FN).

Three names, one metric: sensitivity, recall and true positive rate (TPR). Its denominator is the top row of the matrix.

Specificity

Of the patients who did not have the outcome, the share the model correctly left alone: TN ÷ (TN + FP).

Its denominator is the bottom row of the matrix.

Precision

Of the patients the model flagged, the share who actually had the outcome: TP ÷ (TP + FP).

It shares TP with sensitivity but not the denominator: everyone flagged, not everyone with the outcome. It falls when the outcome is rarer.

False positive rate (FPR)

Of the patients who did not have the outcome, the share who got a false alarm: FP ÷ (FP + TN). FPR = 1 − specificity.

FPR is not “the share of flags that were wrong”; that is 1 − precision, with a different denominator.

F1

The harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity: high only when both are high.

What F1 leaves out: it ignores the true negatives, and it treats a missed case and a false alarm as equally costly.

Undefined metric

A metric whose denominator is 0, such as precision when no one is flagged (0 ÷ 0). Show it as “undefined”, never as 0.

m11 returns null (JavaScript) or None (Python) for these.

Thresholds

Flagged per 100

How many patients out of every 100 the model flags at a threshold: (TP + FP) ÷ all × 100. It is the workload the flag creates.

The lecture uses calls per 100 discharges to pick a threshold for a team's capacity.

Threshold for the job

A threshold chosen with the people who act on the flag: their capacity, what a missed case costs, or a target sensitivity.

The best cut-off depends on the job, not on accuracy. The most accurate thresholds often flag almost no one.

Ranking and probabilities

ROC curve

Every threshold at once: each one gives a (false positive rate, true positive rate) point, and the curve joins them.

A useful model bows toward the top-left. The diagonal is random ranking: it catches patients with and without the outcome at the same rate.

AUROC

The area under the ROC curve: the chance that a random patient with the outcome gets a higher probability than a random patient without it. It describes ranking.

0.5 is random ranking. AUROC is not accuracy, it doesn't choose the threshold, and it doesn't change when you move the threshold. There is no universal good value: it depends on the job.

Models

Prevalence baseline

A “model” that gives every patient the same probability: the share of positives in its training rows. AUROC 0.5.

The lecture draws it as the chance line. In scikit-learn: DummyClassifier(strategy="prior").

Logistic regression

A weighted sum of the features, squeezed into a probability between 0 and 1 by an S-shaped curve.

A classifier despite its name. Simple and checkable; it misses curved or combined effects unless you add them.

Random forest

Many decision trees, each grown on a resample of the patients and choosing each split from a random subset of features; the probability is the average of the trees' probabilities.

Here: 300 trees, at least 3 training patients per leaf.

Gradient boosting

Trees added in sequence, each one learning a correction to the current model's score that reduces its loss.

Corrections add up on the log-odds scale and can lower a prediction as well as raise it; the final score becomes a probability. Here: LightGBM with its default settings, as in the lecture.

k-nearest neighbours (kNN)

A patient's probability is the share of positives among the k most similar training patients. Here k = 25.

“Similar” means close in distance across all features, so features are scaled first: scaling stops a feature's units from dominating the distance, but doesn't make every feature equally informative. Small k follows individual patients closely; large k smooths.

Pipeline

One object that holds every step that learns from data (encoding, filling in missing values, scaling) plus the model, so all of them learn from the training rows only.

In scikit-learn: make_pipeline(...).

Scaling

Putting every feature on the same scale (here: subtract the training mean, divide by the training standard deviation).

kNN uses distances, so a feature measured in large units would otherwise dominate. The scaler must learn its means from training rows only, inside the pipeline.

Imputation

Filling in missing values. Here each blank becomes the median of that feature in the training rows.

Learned from data, so it belongs inside the pipeline.

Training and testing

Training set

The 80% of rows the models learn from. Encoding, imputation and scaling also learn from these rows only.

Marked split = train in each CSV.

Test set

The 20% of rows no model learns from. Scoring the models on them shows how they do on patients they never saw.

Marked split = test in each CSV. Every choice made by looking at it makes its score more optimistic.

Training AUROC

AUROC on the patients a model learned from. It flatters every model.

Compare it with the test AUROC: a big gap means the model memorised its training patients.

Overfitting

A model that learns its training patients too closely, noise included, so it scores far better on them than on new patients.

The sign is a big gap between training and test AUROC. A random forest can rank its own training patients almost perfectly and new ones no better than logistic regression.

Five rules for a fair comparison

Same split for every model; preprocessing inside a pipeline; no leaked columns; each patient's records kept together; no model fitted on the test rows.

Without them, the “winner” may just be the model that saw more of the answers. None of these mistakes raises an error.

Leaked column

A column derived from, or recorded after, the outcome. It gives the model the answer, so the score looks far better than it will be on new patients.

Example: HbA1c defines the diabetes label. Check every column's “When is it known?” entry against the prediction point.

When is it known?

The data dictionary's entry saying when each value exists, relative to the moment the prediction is made.

A predictor must be known at the prediction point. Anything known only at or after the outcome is a leak.

Records kept together

If a patient has several records, all of them go in the same set, training or test. Otherwise the model is tested on people it has already seen.

Each dataset here has one row per person, so this rule is met by design.

Peeking

Setting a threshold, a model or any other choice while looking at the test patients. It promises a result you won't get on new patients.

In a real project, choices are made on separate data before the test set is used.

This activity

Clinical lead

Your app's reader: someone who knows the clinical problem but not the metrics, and who must decide whether to use a model.

Write for them: plain words, every metric's denominator said in patients, and every number labelled with where it comes from.

DECISIONS.md

The file where you write what matters for each job before you see the results, and your recommendations and one design choice after.

python -m analysis copies it to results/decisions.json, so your app can show your recommendations.

Results files

The JSON files in results/ that the Python workflow writes. Your app reads only these.

Commit and push results/ so your hosted app can load them.

Public app link

A web address anyone can open without signing in, such as https://judge-the-model.streamlit.app/ or https://username.github.io/judge-the-model/.

Test it in a private or incognito window.

Public GitHub repository

Your code, data, results and DECISIONS.md, pushed to GitHub. It is your backup and what you submit.

A commit on your laptop isn't a backup until it's pushed. Your host (Streamlit Community Cloud or GitHub Pages) also runs your app from it.
